# E5 — The complete localiser on the robot

*M2 · 180 minutes of a 180-minute session · 130 points · file `student/mcl_template.py`*

Command that decides it: `./tools/run_lab.sh grade --task mcl_production,mcl_wide,mcl_budget,mcl_dirty --controller student/mcl_template.py --headless`

The filter, on the robot, in four graded drives: localise from a 0.5 m prior; the same drive from a 2 m prior, where the true pose starts out surrounded by a thousand wrong neighbours; the same accuracy on a quarter of the particles and every beam; and the same hall with a dirty LIDAR window, where the model has to be told what the sensor actually measures. One function is missing from the template — the sensor model — and it is the one that decides which hypothesis the weights prefer.

The tasks are graded by the simulator's own grader, the same program that produced the numbers in `docs/exercises.md`, and the shipped template fails all four — on accuracy everywhere, and on NEES in the dirty-LIDAR task. NEES is the mean normalised squared error: the squared error divided by the variance you claimed, averaged over the drive, and 1.0 means the σ you published is the error you make. It is the only criterion in this laboratory that grades the honesty of a number rather than its size, and it is what the two-minute viva conversation is about.

The σ you report comes out of the particles, so it is the spread of the cloud and nothing else. Do not widen it to pass the band: `student/FAILURE.md` measures what the shipped template does — an estimate 0.68 m out behind a σ wide enough to score a NEES of **0.12**, which misses L4's band [0.3, 5] **from below**. A ratio can be satisfied from the wrong side, and widening a σ until the number falls into the band is the same trick with more steps.

## Do this, in this order

1. `./tools/run_lab.sh sim` first and drive the L1 course by hand while the estimate is on the screen, so the thing you are grading is visible before you touch a parameter.
2. `TODO(L1)`, the beam model: log-likelihood per beam, the four mixture terms, the beam stride and α in the sheet's ranges. `./tools/run_lab.sh grade` from the first minute, because the failure trace tells you which of the four terms is wrong and there is no other way to find out.
3. The parameters that the sheet asks you to sweep, one at a time, with the measured outcome written into the protocol table as you go. The two that matter most in a hall with few landmarks are the mixture α and the motion noise rate, and the reason is the same reason as in E4's spread criterion.
4. L4 is a map that is wrong by one wall. Watch the estimate there and be ready to explain what you see: the robot knows where it is and the map disagrees, and the shape of the resulting particle cloud is the answer to the viva's last question.

## Protocol — what goes in the write-up

* The four graded tasks: the grader's own table — RMSE, improvement, maximum error, NEES, rate, contacts — with your parameter values next to it. This is the deliverable; it is generated by `./tools/run_lab.sh grade`, not written out.
* L1 with 30, 100, 300, 1000 and 3000 particles and α at 0.01, 0.05 and 0.15, as a table of RMSE and NEES: the cost curve of a filter, and where the recovery time constant stops improving.
* L3's 10 s outage as two curves against drive time: the GPS error and your error. The point where they cross is the sentence to write.
* L4: a map of the map's error and a plot of your particle cloud at the moment of maximum disagreement — three frames, with the cloud's shape described in words.
* The σ against the actual error over one drive, as one plot with both curves, and the mean of the squared ratio. That is the NEES in the handout, and it has to be your number.

## Hand in

* All four tasks PASSing, one `./tools/run_lab.sh grade` line each, with the parameter values that got them there.
* The NEES plot and the paragraph about it: which of the σ's two jobs (the estimator's and the confidence interval's) your filter does better, and what you changed to find out.
